Lecture 7 — Bias, Variance, and Regularization
CS 471: Introduction to Machine Learning
Prof. M. Karimi · Spring 2026

[00:00] Good morning. Today we will tie together the three concepts that you've
been seeing in your homework: bias, variance, and the regularization techniques
we use to balance them. By the end of this lecture you should be able to look
at a learning curve and tell me, just by inspecting the gap between training
and validation error, whether your model is suffering from high bias or high
variance — and what to do about it.

[02:30] Let's start with the definitions. The bias of an estimator is the
expected difference between the prediction and the true value, averaged over
many training sets drawn from the same distribution. A linear model trying to
fit a curved relationship has high bias — no matter how many samples we throw
at it, it cannot represent the true function. The variance, in contrast, is
how much the prediction wiggles when we resample the training set. A deep tree
that perfectly fits 500 training points will produce wildly different
predictions if we shuffle the data and retrain.

[06:15] The famous bias-variance decomposition tells us that the expected
squared error of a model on a new point can be written as the sum of three
terms: bias squared, variance, and the irreducible noise of the data itself.
We cannot remove the noise — that's the floor. But we can trade bias against
variance by changing model capacity. A simple model has high bias, low
variance. A flexible model has low bias, high variance. The sweet spot lives
in the middle, and finding it is mostly what we mean by "model selection."

[12:00] How do we diagnose where we are on this curve? Plot training error and
validation error against the number of training examples. If both errors are
high and close together, you are bias-limited — your model is too simple.
Adding data will not help; you need a richer model. If training error is low
but validation error is much higher, you are variance-limited — your model is
memorizing. More data, more regularization, or simpler models will help.

[18:45] Now, regularization. The simplest form, called L2 or ridge
regularization, adds a penalty proportional to the sum of squared weights to
the loss function. It shrinks every weight toward zero, which reduces
variance because the model becomes less sensitive to any one feature. The
strength of the penalty is the hyperparameter lambda. Small lambda — almost
no penalty, you get the unregularized solution. Large lambda — every weight
collapses to zero and the model just predicts the mean. You tune lambda by
cross-validation.

[25:00] L1, or lasso, regularization adds a penalty proportional to the sum
of absolute values of the weights. The geometry is different — the penalty
surface has corners — and at those corners weights are pushed exactly to
zero rather than just close to zero. This makes lasso a feature-selection
tool. If you have a thousand candidate features and you suspect only twenty
matter, lasso will tell you which twenty.

[31:20] Elastic net is the convex combination of L1 and L2. You get the
sparsity of lasso with the stability of ridge when features are correlated.
In practice, for tabular data, elastic net is a strong default — start there
before reaching for fancier methods.

[35:00] Dropout, which we will study in detail next week, is a kind of
regularization for neural networks. At training time, each forward pass
randomly drops some fraction of the units. The network has to learn
representations that don't depend on any single neuron. The dropout rate
is your lambda — typical values are 0.1 to 0.5.

[40:30] Early stopping is also regularization in disguise. As you train
longer, training error keeps falling but validation error eventually rises —
that's the signature of overfitting. If you stop at the validation minimum,
you have implicitly chosen a model with bounded effective capacity. Almost
every modern training pipeline uses early stopping with a patience parameter.

[46:00] Data augmentation — flipping, cropping, color-jittering images,
synonym-replacement on text — also reduces variance. You're effectively
making your training set look bigger without actually collecting new data,
which is the cheapest variance reduction available.

[51:30] Practical recipe for the homework. Start simple. Train a baseline.
Plot the learning curves. Read the gap between training and validation. If
the gap is small but error is high, increase capacity — deeper net, more
features, polynomial expansion. If the gap is large, regularize harder, get
more data, or simplify. Iterate.

[56:00] One last thing. None of this is theoretical decoration. Every model
you ship in industry — a fraud detector, a recommender, a medical risk
score — sits somewhere on the bias-variance curve, and the difference
between a model that works in production and one that doesn't is usually
measured in how well its designers found that sweet spot.
